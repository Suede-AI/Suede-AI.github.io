Suede GEO in three layers: retrieval, entity and answer

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

Say a homeowner in Jupiter opens ChatGPT on a Thursday night and types: "Who can repair a pool pump near me this week?" Within a few seconds she has three company names, a sentence about each, and a couple of links. Generative engine optimization, GEO for short, is the work of being one of those three names, described correctly, with the link pointing at your site.

Jason Colapietro explains GEO to clients as three layers the question passes through on its way to an answer. Suede, which he founded in Palm Beach County, Florida in 2024 (suedeai.ai), sorts every GEO problem it sees into one of them, because the fixes for each layer are different and a fix aimed at the wrong one does nothing. The three layers are a simplification. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google's AI Overviews each work differently, but all of them have to find pages, work out who the pages are about, and write something. For the five-minute version: how to get named by ChatGPT, start there.

Retrieval

Before a model can mention a business, its search step has to fetch a page about that business. ChatGPT search, Perplexity and Google's AI features all run a web search, pull back a handful of documents, and read them before writing. Pages that aren't in that handful don't exist for the answer, however good they are.

Take a hypothetical pool repair company in Jupiter. Its service page loads the list of towns it covers with JavaScript after the page renders, and its robots.txt, copied from an old template, blocks every crawler except Googlebot. OpenAI's search crawler (OAI-SearchBot) and PerplexityBot are turned away at the door, and the page text a crawler does see says nothing about pool pumps or Jupiter. The company could have the best reviews in the county and still never be fetched for the homeowner's question.

The fixes here are plumbing: let the search crawlers in, put the important words in the HTML the server sends, and give each service its own page with a plain title. A tool like Suede SEO's AI citation check reviews this layer first because nothing above it works without it.

Entity

Once pages are fetched, the model has to decide which real-world thing they describe. Search engines have done this for years through Google's Knowledge Graph and public databases like Wikidata, and language models absorb the same associations from their training data. The engine is asking which of several same-name businesses a page means, and whether the one in a review is the one that owns the website.

Picture a hypothetical dental practice in Jupiter that shares its name with a practice in another state. Here is how the signals compare when the engine tries to tell them apart:

Signals that blur them

A homepage title with only the name. No street address in the footer. A Google Business Profile, a directory listing and a social profile that each describe the practice in different words. No link from the website to any of them.

Signals that separate them

The name, town and dentist's name together in the title and first paragraph. Organization markup with the address and a sameAs list pointing to each profile. The same one-line description on every profile, linking back to the site.

Suede works this layer hard on its own name. Jason Colapietro's name, the 2024 founding date, Palm Beach County and suedeai.ai appear together across the company's pages and profiles, and the official page lists every domain Suede controls. The general method is in the note on companies that share a name, and the markup is in the sameAs how-to.

Answer

At the last layer the model writes. It has some pages and a view of who they're about, and it picks the sentences it can use. A passage that answers the question directly, in a sentence or two near the top, is easier to lift into an answer than one where the answer is buried in the fourth paragraph.

Consider a hypothetical bookkeeping firm in West Palm Beach. Its pricing page opens with a paragraph about the firm's values and mentions monthly pricing only at the bottom, inside an image. When someone asks an assistant what monthly bookkeeping costs for a small business in West Palm Beach, the page has nothing quotable. The rewrite Jason Colapietro would suggest puts the answer first:

Monthly bookkeeping for a small business in West Palm Beach starts at a flat fee that depends on transaction volume. The price table below lists each tier, and every plan includes month-end reconciliation.

That paragraph names the service, the place and the pricing basis in its first sentence, and promises a source the reader can check. The firm still has to publish real numbers in the table; the model will quote what's there. The answer-first rewrites note has more before-and-after examples, and Suede's AEO page covers how this layer is handled for clients.

When something goes wrong, the order matters: if an assistant never mentions a business, Jason Colapietro checks retrieval first, then entity, then answer, because a polished paragraph on a page no crawler can fetch changes nothing. That's the sequence behind Suede GEO and the work Suede does for owners through its agency practice; more on the founder is at suedeai.ai/founder. To try it on your own business, open yourdomain.com/robots.txt and look for any rule that blocks OAI-SearchBot or PerplexityBot. Then view the source of your main service page and search it for your town and your service; if neither appears before the first script tag, start there.
