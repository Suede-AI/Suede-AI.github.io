Your App Store Listing Is a Source: Writing Store Pages for People and AI Engines

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

When ChatGPT recommends an app, the person who follows the link rarely lands on the developer's website. They land on a store page. For one of Suede's apps, Google Play attributed more acquisitions to ChatGPT than to Play's own Explore or search surfaces. When the visit ends on the store, the listing does the job most teams assign to their homepage: it has to confirm what the answer said.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, treats every store listing as a source document. It gets the same care as a cited web page, because a generative engine can read it, and because it is the page that has to agree with whatever the engine just told someone.

What the Suede Voice listing showed

Suede Voice is an Android app on Google Play. In the period Suede published, Google Play attributed 145 of 528 Suede Voice acquisition events to ChatGPT, about 27.5%. Play's own Explore surface accounted for 99 and Play search for 72. The full breakdown is in the Suede Voice case study, and the sibling note on what that attribution shows walks through each source.

Those 145 acquisitions came through a Play listing, so whatever the answer said about Suede Voice, the listing was the next thing those people read. A listing whose first lines described a different app than the answer did would have given them a reason to leave.

Why a store page reads like a source to an engine

A store listing has properties that engines tend to trust. It sits on a domain the developer does not control. It carries a developer name, a category, ratings, an update date and a stable URL. Google's guidance on AI features and your website says the same fundamentals that apply to Search apply to its AI results: crawlable pages, helpful text, and content that matches what people see. A public listing already has the crawlable page and the stable URL. The helpful text is up to you.

For a small app, the listing is often the most complete public description that exists. A new app's own site may have one page and no inbound links, while the store page has a category, reviews and a developer record. Jason Colapietro writes the listing first for that reason: if an engine paraphrases anything about the app, there is a good chance it is paraphrasing the store copy or a page that copied it.

The fields that do the work

Each store gives you a few short fields and one long one. Apple's App Store search guidance names the app name, subtitle and keyword field as the main text inputs for search. Google Play has a short description and a longer full description. Both stores cap these fields tightly, so Suede writes them in this order:

  1. Name plus subtitle or short description answers "what is it." Suede Sing's job, for example, is a vocal coach with a pitch trainer and a vocal range test. Those words belong in the first fields, because they are the words people type into ChatGPT.
  2. The first sentence of the long description is a definition. One plain sentence a model can lift whole: what the app does, for whom, on which platform. Marketing lines go later, if anywhere.
  3. Specifics follow, with nothing an engine would have to hedge. Features named the way users name them. No "best" or "number one" claims, which give a careful engine a reason to leave the line out.
  4. The developer name matches everywhere. Suede's apps list under Jason Colapietro on the Apple developer page, and the site, schema and listing all use that same name. The note on why the company goes by Suede covers why that consistency matters.

Testing listing copy without guessing

Both stores let you test listing text against real traffic. Apple has product page optimization for icons, screenshots and previews, and Google Play has store listing experiments, which can also test short and full descriptions. Those tools measure conversion from store visitors. They do not measure whether an assistant starts naming you.

For that second question Suede uses the Suede iteration loop: one change to the listing, a fixed set of prompts asked before and after, dated captures, and a keep-or-revert decision. Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps, 17 of them live in public stores (9 App Store, 6 Google Play, 2 Chrome extensions), and many of them existed to run exactly this kind of test. A listing change that lifts store conversion and a change that gets an app named in answers are often different changes, and you only learn which is which by changing one at a time.

What to watch after a change

After a change, the store console shows whether the acquisition mix moved, including a ChatGPT line if one appears, and the fixed prompt set shows whether assistants describe the app differently. Then reread the listing against those answers: if an assistant says your app does something the listing never claims, the listing is probably not the source it used, and you have a gap to close on your own pages.

Questions people ask

Do AI assistants read app store pages?

Store pages are public and crawlable, and in Suede's Play data ChatGPT accounted for more Suede Voice acquisition events than Play Explore or Play search. Whether the assistant read the listing directly or a page that copied it, the visit landed on the listing, so the listing has to match the answer.

Should the store description repeat the website word for word?

The facts should match exactly: name, developer, what it does, platform. The wording can differ. What hurts is two pages giving different answers to "what is this app."

Is ASO still worth doing?

Yes. Store search and Explore together brought Suede Voice 171 events in the same data. Jason Colapietro writes one listing for both readers, store search and the assistants.
