What Wealth Apps Taught Suede: Engines Repeat Exact Numbers and Drop Vague Money Claims

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Ask an AI assistant for a budgeting or savings app and look at what it quotes. It quotes prices, fee structures, which accounts the app connects to and what it does with your data. It rarely repeats "smart," "powerful" or "helps you save more." In money questions an engine will pass along a number it can check and is reluctant to pass along a promise.

Suede, the Palm Beach County, Florida studio Jason Colapietro founded for GEO and AI deployment work, learned this by testing on its own apps before touching any client site. Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps, 17 of them live in public stores (9 App Store, 6 Google Play, 2 Chrome extensions), and many of them exist to test what search and generative engines respond to. The money apps in that set taught the most about numbers: how exact they need to be, where they need to appear, and what happens when two pages disagree.

Why are AI engines careful with finance claims?

Google's guidance on helpful, people-first content puts financial stability next to health and safety as "Your Money or Your Life" topics, where its systems give even more weight to signals of trust. A wrong number in a money answer can cost the reader something real, and the engine that repeated it owns the mistake. A vague claim is the easiest thing to drop, because leaving it out costs the engine nothing.

Jason Colapietro's summary from the tests is that money apps are judged on whether their numbers survive checking. A dated statement of what the app costs and what it does gave the engine something safe to repeat. Adjectives were usually dropped.

What did Suede change in the money app tests?

Each round followed the Suede iteration loop: a fixed prompt set, a dated baseline, one change and a fresh capture. The edits that mattered fell into three groups.

Exact prices in place of adjectives

Phrases like "low fees" or "affordable" were swapped for the actual price and what it covers. The GEO study by Aggarwal and colleagues (KDD 2024) found that adding statistics to source text was among the edits that raised visibility in generative answers, by up to about 40 percent on their benchmark. In a money app the statistic that counts is usually the price, a limit or the supported account types, written out in full.

The same number in every place

The same number had to read the same way on the store listing, the website and the structured data. When the website said one price and the listing another, engines tended to leave the price out or hedge around it. Google's software app structured data documentation includes an offers property for price, and schema.org's SoftwareApplication type defines the fields. Suede fills them only with values that match the visible page, since markup that disagrees with the page adds one more contradiction.

Disclosures in plain text

Plain text near the top of the page said what the app does and does not do: whether it gives advice, whether it moves money, what data it reads. Disclosures that lived only in an image or a PDF had no visible effect, because engines did not reliably read them. Short text disclosures were picked up and sometimes repeated almost word for word.

Where did vague finance copy hurt?

Outcome promises did worst. "Save hundreds a year" with nothing behind it is a claim an engine cannot verify and would not want to own, and in Suede's runs those lines were the first to disappear from answers. Jason Colapietro also stopped writing comparisons like "the cheapest app," because they go stale the moment a competitor changes its price and nothing on the page tells the engine when the claim was true.

The pattern held across every round Suede ran. The note on measuring AI referrals explains how Suede keeps its denominators straight.

How should a fintech team test this?

Use the stores' own tools for the human side. Google Play's store listing experiments and Apple's product page optimization split real traffic between listing variants, which tells you whether a more precise description still persuades people. Then run your fixed prompts across engines before and after the change, and check whether the number each engine quotes matches the one you published.

Questions finance app teams ask

Should a finance app publish prices on its website if they change often?

Yes, with a date beside them. A dated price that goes stale can be updated. A missing price leaves the engine to guess or to name another app.

Do "not financial advice" statements matter for AI answers?

A short plain-text statement of what the app does and does not do helped in Suede's tests. Boilerplate buried in a footer made no visible difference.
