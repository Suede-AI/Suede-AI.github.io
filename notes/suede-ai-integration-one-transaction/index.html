Suede AI Integration Traced Through One Transaction: A Lead Email From Inbox to Booked Job

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

A little after seven on a Tuesday morning, an email reaches a pool service company in Palm Beach County. Call the company Example Pools; it is hypothetical, and so is everything that follows. The sender has a green pool, a party on Saturday and a question about whether anyone can come out this week. In most small service businesses that email waits until somebody opens the inbox between jobs.

Jason Colapietro uses this kind of message to explain what an AI integration involves. He founded Suede in Palm Beach County, Florida, in 2024; at suedeai.ai the company sells this work as Suede AI integration, and most of it is wiring: getting one message to move through five or six systems without being lost, duplicated or answered wrongly. Here is the email's whole trip, one system at a time, with what goes wrong at each stop.

The trip, step by step

  1. The email lands in the shared inbox. With Google Workspace, the integration can subscribe to Gmail push notifications, so it hears about the message within moments instead of checking on a timer.

    If this step fails

    Push notifications can arrive late, twice or not at all. Keep a slow polling job as a backstop, and record each message ID as it is processed so a second notification for the same email is ignored.

  2. The agent sorts the message: new lead, existing customer, vendor, or junk. This one is a new lead.

    If this step fails

    A lead sorted as junk is the costliest mistake in the whole chain, because nobody sees it. Anything the agent is unsure about goes to a review queue, and the agent is never allowed to delete mail.

  3. The agent pulls out the fields the business needs: name, address, phone, service wanted and how urgent it is. It returns them as structured data, checked against a fixed schema.

    If this step fails

    Models will sometimes fill a missing field with something plausible. Validate what comes back (a phone number in a real format, an address inside the service area), leave a field empty when the email doesn't contain it, and let the reply ask for what is missing.

  4. The CRM gets a lookup, then a write. If the email address or phone already belongs to a contact, the lead attaches to that record; otherwise a new contact and a new deal are created, tagged with the source "email."

    If this step fails

    The usual result is a duplicate contact, from a retry or a second notification. Use the email's message ID as an idempotency key for the write, and queue retries when the CRM's API rate limit pushes back.

  5. The calendar is checked. The integration asks Google Calendar for free and busy times for the technician who covers that ZIP code, and picks two openings before Saturday.

    If this step fails

    Availability can change between the check and the booking. Hold the two slots as tentative events and confirm the one the customer picks at the moment they pick it.

  6. The owner approves. The agent drafts a reply offering the two times and sends the draft to the owner's phone with an approve button. For a first contact with a new customer, nothing goes out without a person saying yes.

    If this step fails

    The owner is on a roof and doesn't see it. Send a reminder, then hand the draft to whoever is covering the office. The draft never promotes itself to sent.

  7. The reply goes out in the same email thread, and the CRM records what was sent and when.

    If this step fails

    The send works and the CRM write fails, so the record says nobody answered. Write the log entry as pending before sending, mark it sent afterward, and run a nightly job that finds anything stuck in pending.

  8. The customer picks Thursday. The tentative hold becomes a booking, the other hold is released, and the deal moves forward with its original source still attached, so the owner's reporting can show the job started as an email.

    If this step fails

    If the source was not stamped back at step 4, it is gone by now, and the job shows up in reports as "unknown." Attribution has to be recorded at the first write, because nothing later in the chain knows where the lead came from.

What the trip shows

Count the model's share: it sorts the email at step 2, extracts fields at step 3 and drafts a reply at step 6. Everything else is plumbing between ordinary business software, and every failure path above except the junk filter and the invented field is a plumbing problem. That matches how Jason Colapietro describes AI engineering generally, and the case is argued at length in the software-first essay. Once a flow like this is live, the first-week deployment list covers what to watch while real customers start using it.

The approval step is the one owners tend to want to remove once the drafts look good. Jason Colapietro's advice is to keep it for new customers and loosen it only for message types whose drafts have been reviewed for a while. That call belongs to the owner, because it depends on what a wrong reply would cost the business.

Take one real inquiry from last week and trace it on paper through your own systems, the way this page does: where it arrived, who touched it, which tools it passed through, and where it could have been lost. The step where nobody can say what happened is the place to start.
