Crawler Access for GEO: robots.txt, OAI-SearchBot and llms.txt, Sorted by What Mattered

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Crawler access is the cheapest part of GEO to get right and the easiest to get wrong without noticing. One line in robots.txt can keep a page out of ChatGPT's search results, and nothing on the site will look broken. Meanwhile, teams spend afternoons on files that no engine has said it reads.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, sorts crawler work into two bins. Gates are settings that decide whether an engine can use a page at all, and the vendors document them. Placebos are things that feel like progress but have no documented effect, and they stay in that bin until a test shows otherwise. This note goes through both, using the public documentation and the way Suede runs its app tests.

Which crawler are you actually blocking?

A mistake Jason Colapietro looks for first in any audit is treating "AI bots" as one thing. OpenAI documents three separate agents. OAI-SearchBot finds pages to show in ChatGPT search results. GPTBot collects content that may be used to train models. ChatGPT-User fetches a page when a person in ChatGPT asks for it. Each has its own robots.txt token, so you can allow one and block another.

For a business that wants to be cited, that split decides most of the outcome. A site that blocks GPTBot to keep its content out of training has made a reasonable choice about training. It has not blocked ChatGPT search, as long as OAI-SearchBot is still allowed. A site that copies a "block all AI" snippet from a forum and includes OAI-SearchBot has opted out of being shown in ChatGPT search, and OpenAI says so in plain language on that page.

Perplexity's crawler page draws the same line between PerplexityBot, which indexes pages for its answers, and Perplexity-User, which fetches when a person asks. Both vendors describe the user-triggered agent separately from the crawler, and robots.txt does not apply to the two in the same way, so read their pages before assuming a block holds.

The gates, in the order Suede checks them

  1. robots.txt allows the search crawlers by name, or does not mention them under a blanket disallow. Google's robots.txt introduction is the clearest explanation of how groups and user-agent matching work, and the same rules apply to every crawler that honors the file.
  2. The page loads for a crawler without a login, a cookie wall or a client-side script that has to run before any text appears.
  3. The sentence you want quoted is in the served HTML. An app landing page whose description arrives through JavaScript after load gives a crawler less to work with than a plain paragraph.
  4. Each page has one stable, canonical URL. Answer engines link to pages, and a page that moves breaks the links they already hold.

Each of these is a yes or a no, and a failure at any one makes everything downstream irrelevant. That is why Jason Colapietro puts them first in every client review at Suede's agency practice.

Where llms.txt fits

The llms.txt proposal suggests a Markdown file at the site root that summarizes a site for language models and points to clean versions of key pages. It is a sensible idea, and this site publishes one. The proposal is a proposal, though. Google's page on AI features and your website says you do not need new machine-readable files or special markup to appear in its AI results, and the crawler pages above describe robots.txt, not llms.txt.

So Suede files llms.txt under "placebo until tested." It costs little to publish and it is useful as a human-readable map. Suede puts client effort into the gates that decide citation first. In the Suede iteration loop, a change earns a "keep" only when the fixed prompt set shows a different answer after the change and the change was the only thing that moved. Suede keeps a tactic once the prompt set isolates its effect, and llms.txt stays in the placebo column until it does. The note on experiments that did nothing covers how Suede records tactics like this.

Why app tests made this clearer

Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps, many of them to test what SEO and GEO respond to. App landing pages are small, so a crawler problem shows up fast: if a page is blocked or empty to a bot, there is nothing else on the site for an engine to fall back on. On a large client site, a blocked section can hide behind hundreds of pages that still work. That is part of why Suede runs these questions on apps first, a choice explained in apps as a GEO laboratory.

Questions people ask

If I block GPTBot, will ChatGPT stop citing me?

Per OpenAI's documentation, GPTBot is the training crawler. ChatGPT search results rely on OAI-SearchBot, which has its own token. Check that your rules treat them separately.

Should I publish llms.txt?

It does no harm and can help people and tools find your best pages. Treat it as housekeeping, and do not expect it to fix a blocked crawler or an empty page.

How do I check what a crawler sees?

Fetch the page with JavaScript disabled and read what is left. If the answer you want quoted is missing, an engine is likely missing it too.
