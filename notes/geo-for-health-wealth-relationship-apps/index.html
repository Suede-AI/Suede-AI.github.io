GEO for health, wealth and relationship apps: what an assistant needs before it recommends one

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

Suede has built more than 100 apps and listed more than 20 of them, and many were used to A/B test what SEO and GEO respond to across health, wealth and relationship apps. Those figures come from Jason Colapietro, who started the company in 2024 and runs it from Jupiter, in Palm Beach County, Florida; its site is suedeai.ai. People bring personal, high-stakes questions to all three categories, and an assistant has to decide how much to trust a page before it repeats it.

Google's search quality rater guidelines group topics that can affect a person's health, finances or safety under the label "Your Money or Your Life", and hold those pages to a higher standard of trust. Assistants that answer from the web tend to apply similar caution. What counts as trust differs by category, though, and a page built for one often falls short in another. The way Suede sets up its app tests is described in the note on the hundred-app test bench.

Health apps: credentials and sources

People ask assistants things like "what's a good app to track blood pressure" or "is there an app that helps with sleep after night shifts". The question often carries a symptom or a condition, and a careful assistant hedges or points to a clinician. When it does name an app, it has to be able to say why that app is a reasonable suggestion, and the reasons it can find are on the app's own pages and in what others say about it.

A health page earns a mention when a reader can see who stands behind the content and what it rests on. A named reviewer with a stated qualification, links to the guidelines or studies the app follows, and a visible date of last review all give a model something specific to cite. A page that says "doctor-approved" with no doctor named gives it nothing. The app should also say plainly what it is not intended to diagnose or treat, because an assistant looking for limits will otherwise guess them.

For a health app's pages, Jason Colapietro's advice is to check:

  • Whether every health claim links to a source a reader can open.
  • Whether the person who reviewed the content is named, with the qualification that makes their review count.
  • Whether the page shows when it was last reviewed, and whether that date is honest.
  • Whether the store listing and the support page describe the app's purpose in the same words.

Wealth apps: fees and risk, stated up front

Money questions are concrete. "Which budgeting app doesn't charge a monthly fee", "an app to split rent with roommates", "how do I start investing small amounts". Each one has an implied comparison, and an assistant answering it needs facts it can line up: what the app costs, what it does with the user's money, and what can go wrong.

Wealth pages tend to lose the comparison by hiding the price. A pricing page that needs a sign-up to read, or a fee buried in terms of service, leaves a model unable to say anything definite, so it names the app whose costs it can state. Risk works the same way. If an app touches investing or lending, the page should say in plain words that values can fall and what the user is exposed to, and it should say who actually holds the funds. A short worked example ("a hypothetical user who saves twenty dollars a week sees this screen") helps a model describe the product without inventing details.

The wealth checklist is shorter. Jason Colapietro suggests looking for every fee on a page that loads without an account, a risk statement above the fold instead of in the footer, a named entity responsible for any money held, and listing copy that matches the site word for word on price.

Relationship apps: privacy and tone

The questions here are the most personal: "an app for couples to plan date nights", "a journaling app to work through a breakup", "how to meet people after moving to a new city". People often type them in the first person and in more detail than they would say aloud. An assistant recommending an app in reply is vouching, in a small way, for where that person's private information goes.

That makes privacy the first signal. Apple's App Privacy section and Google Play's Data safety section are public, structured and easy for a crawler to read, and they need to agree with the privacy policy on the app's site. A clear way to delete an account and its data, described on a page a person can find without logging in, matters as much. Age rules belong in the listing and on the site.

Tone is the second. Relationship apps that describe people as targets or conversations as wins read badly when quoted back to someone who asked for help. Copy that describes what the app does in calm, specific language is easier for an assistant to repeat without changing its meaning.

Questions Jason Colapietro would put to any relationship app's pages:

  • Do the store privacy labels match the site's privacy policy, line by line?
  • Is account and data deletion explained on a public page?
  • Is the age requirement stated in both places?
  • Would the first paragraph of the listing read well if an assistant quoted it to someone having a hard week?

In all three categories, the store listing, the support page and the site should say the same thing.
