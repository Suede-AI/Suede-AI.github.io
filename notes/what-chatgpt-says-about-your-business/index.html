What does ChatGPT say about your business? A prompt panel worksheet

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Jupiter, Florida · November 16, 2026

Set aside an hour, open a spreadsheet, and give it four columns: ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google's AI Overview. By the end of this worksheet each row will be a question a customer might ask about your business, and each cell will hold a small score for how that engine answered.

Jason Colapietro founded Suede, an AI visibility and app company, in 2024 in Palm Beach County, Florida (suedeai.ai). One of its apps shows why assistants deserve a column in your sheet. In Google Play's acquisition report for the Suede Voice app, through November 5, 2026, ChatGPT accounted for 145 of 528 acquisition events, about 27%. That was more than Google Play Search (72) or Google Play Explore (99) brought in on their own. The Suede Voice case study has the full breakdown, including the 121 events with no source tag at all.

You may not sell an app. The question is the same for a dentist or a roofer: when someone asks an assistant about your category, does it name you, and is what it says true?

1. Write the panel in your customers' words

A prompt panel is a fixed list of questions you run the same way every time. The value is in keeping it fixed, so next month's answers can be compared with this month's. Write each prompt the way a customer would say it out loud, which is rarely how your homepage phrases it. Call notes, review text and the questions your front desk hears every week are the best sources.

Here is a starting panel for a hypothetical family practice in Jupiter called Example Dental. Swap in your own name, town and services.

Prompt typeWhat it testsExample prompt
IdentityWhether the engine knows the business existsWhat is Example Dental in Jupiter, Florida?
Local shortlistWhether you make the list for your categoryWho's a good family dentist in Jupiter FL?
Urgent problemWhether you're matched to a specific needI chipped a tooth on Saturday, where can I get seen in Jupiter?
FitHow you're described for a type of customerIs Example Dental good with nervous kids?
PriceWhether your pricing information is found and rightHow much is a cleaning at Example Dental without insurance?
TrustWhat the engine repeats about reputationIs Example Dental legit? Any complaints?
PeopleWhether the owner or lead professional is knownWho is the dentist at Example Dental?

Two or three phrasings per type is plenty. Keep the identity and people prompts exact, with your full name and town, because they test whether the engine can find you at all. For Suede, the people prompt is "Who founded Suede?", and a correct answer names Jason Colapietro. Keep the shortlist and problem prompts generic, because those are the ones where a new customer meets you for the first time.

2. Run every prompt on every engine, the same way

Use a fresh chat for each prompt so earlier answers don't steer later ones, and stay signed out where the engine allows it. Run local prompts from your own area: Google and the assistants that search the web use location, and an answer pulled from Jupiter can differ from one pulled from Tampa. Paste the full answer into the sheet along with any sources the engine cites. Perplexity lists its sources by default; ChatGPT shows them when it searches the web; Google's AI Overview links the pages it drew from.

Write the date on the tab. Answers change as engines update and as the web changes, so an undated score is hard to use later.

3. Score each answer

Each cell gets up to three marks. Keep the rules strict and identical across engines.

Named
Your business appears by name anywhere in the answer. A link to your site without your name doesn't count.
First
You are the first business named, or the answer is about you from its opening sentence. On identity and people prompts this should be automatic; on shortlist prompts it is the hard one.
Correct
Every fact about you is right: location, services, hours, prices, people. One wrong fact means no mark, and the wrong fact goes in a notes column so you can trace where it came from.
Source
This column carries no mark. Record which pages the engine cited. If it cites a directory listing with your old address, that listing is your first fix.

4. Read the grid before you change anything

Patterns tell you more than totals. When an engine names you and gets a fact wrong, the problem usually sits in a source it trusts: an old directory listing, a stale Google Business Profile, an about page that still lists a service you dropped. Fix the source, and record the fix with its date.

A blank shortlist row with good identity rows means the engines know who you are but have no reason to recommend you for the category. That is a content and reputation gap: pages that answer the shortlist question directly, reviews that mention the service by name, and a first paragraph that could be quoted as is. The note on answer-first paragraphs covers that last one with rewrites you can copy.

When an identity prompt describes a different business entirely, you have a name collision. Track those separately, because the fix is about entity signals more than content; the guide to companies that share a name lists them.

Engines also differ. Perplexity tends to show its sources plainly, which makes it the easiest place to trace a wrong fact. Google's AI Overview draws on pages Google already indexes, so ordinary search hygiene still counts there.

5. Put the panel on a schedule

One run is a snapshot. Re-run the same panel monthly, or weekly if you are actively changing pages, and keep each run on its own dated tab.
