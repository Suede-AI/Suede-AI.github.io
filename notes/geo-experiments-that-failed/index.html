GEO Experiments That Failed: The Tactics Suede Tested and Stopped Doing

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

The most useful document Suede keeps is a list of things that did not work. Every GEO vendor has a slide of wins. Very few publish what they tried, measured, and then dropped, and that list is what keeps a client's budget away from tactics that only feel productive.

Suede is the GEO and AI deployment studio that Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida. Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps, more than 20 of them listed in public stores, and many existed to test what search and generative engines respond to. Thousands of app iterations ran through the Suede iteration loop: one change, fixed prompts, dated captures before and after. Most rounds ended with nothing to report. This note covers the tactics that went into that pile and stayed there.

There are no percentages here. A null result has no lift to report, and inventing one would defeat the purpose of the list.

Repeating the category term in store descriptions

The oldest SEO instinct is to say the keyword more often. On app listings, Suede tried descriptions that repeated the main category phrase more heavily than a person would write it. AI answers did not start naming the app more often. The listing read worse, which can hurt the human conversion that Apple's product page optimization and Google Play's store listing experiments are built to measure.

Apple's own App Store search guidance points to the app name, subtitle and keyword field as the places to put search terms. Description text that says plainly what the app does and who it is for held up better than text built around repetition. The note on store listings as citation sources covers what to write instead.

Spinning out near-duplicate pages for every phrasing

If engines answer questions, the thinking goes, publish a page for every way someone might ask. Suede tested thin variant pages around a single app's topic. They did not increase how often the app was named, and they diluted the pages that already had links pointing to them.

Google is explicit about the risk. Its spam policies call this scaled content abuse when the pages exist mainly to manipulate rankings. In Suede's tests, one strong page per real question did better than a cluster of weak ones.

Structured data that claimed more than the page showed

SoftwareApplication markup helps engines describe an app correctly, as covered in structured data for apps. The failed version was markup that went further than the visible page: features described only in the JSON-LD, or properties filled in to look complete. It did not improve how AI answers described the apps. Google's software app structured data documentation lists the properties it supports, and markup that drifts from what users can see on the page is a policy problem as well as a wasted effort.

What worked was boring by comparison. The markup matched the page, the page matched the store listing, and all three used the same name.

Treating llms.txt as the fix

The llms.txt proposal suggests a Markdown file at the site root that summarizes a site for language models. This site publishes one. As a standalone change on lab apps, adding the file did not move how answers named or described them. It is a proposal, and the documented crawler controls that engines publish are robots.txt and their own user agents. The note on crawler access goes into what did matter there.

Suede keeps the file because it costs little and may help some tools read the site. It does not sell it to clients as a visibility fix.

Talking to the chatbot about yourself

A common belief is that you can teach an assistant about your product by asking it questions, correcting it, or rating its answers. Suede tried this during early tests. Answers in fresh sessions did not change. Asking about yourself is fine for checking what an engine says, which is step two of the loop. As an attempt to influence the answer, it went nowhere.

Why keep a failure log at all?

Because it changes what clients pay for. When a client asks for fifty location pages or a schema overhaul with features they do not have, Jason Colapietro can point to the log and say Suede ran something similar on its own apps first. The path from lab result to client work, including the null results, is in from app lab to client playbook.

The log also keeps the wins honest. When ChatGPT began naming Suede Sing, which by Jason Colapietro's account had never had more than 5 users and then passed 1,000 active users within about a week, Suede could check the change against everything it had already ruled out.

Questions about failed GEO tactics

Does a null result mean a tactic never works?

No. It means it did not work in Suede's tests, on Suede's apps, at that time. Engines change, and a tactic in the log can be retested.

Which failures matter most for a small business?

Mass-produced pages, because they carry a policy risk on top of wasting time. Start with one good page per real question.
