What Health Apps Taught Suede: Caution Is a Ranking Input in AI Answers

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Ask ChatGPT or Perplexity for an app that helps with sleep, stress or a nagging symptom, and the answer comes back careful. It names a few apps, adds a line about seeing a doctor, and leaves out anything that sounds like a cure. That care tells you what the engine is willing to repeat. A health app that wants to be named has to write sentences an engine can pass along without taking on the risk of being wrong about someone's body.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, learned this on its own apps. Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps. 17 are live in public stores: 9 on the Apple App Store, 6 on Google Play and 2 Chrome Web Store extensions. Many exist only to test what search engines and generative engines respond to, across thousands of iterations in health, wealth and relationship apps. The health builds gave the clearest lesson of the three: the version that claimed less, and said where its claims came from, was the version engines described.

Why do AI engines treat health apps differently?

Google's guidance on helpful, people-first content says its systems give even more weight to strong experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust on topics that could significantly affect people's health, financial stability or safety. Google calls these "Your Money or Your Life" topics, and the same page says trust is the most important of the four. Its page on AI features and your website says the usual Search fundamentals apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode. Other engines publish less about how they choose sources, but their answers show the same caution.

So a health app is judged on whether an engine could quote it to a stranger and be comfortable doing so. Jason Colapietro's working rule from the tests: if an engine would need to add a warning after repeating your sentence, it will usually repeat someone else's.

What did Suede change in its health app tests?

Using the Suede iteration loop, each round changed one thing on a store listing or landing page and then re-ran a fixed set of prompts. The health rounds kept coming back to four kinds of edit.

  1. Scope. A broad promise such as "improves your sleep" became a description of the feature, such as "logs bedtime and wake time and shows weekly patterns." An engine can confirm the second sentence by reading the listing.
  2. Sourcing. The guideline or study behind a feature was named and linked on the page. The GEO study by Aggarwal and colleagues (KDD 2024) found that adding citations, quotations and statistics to source text raised its visibility in generative answers by up to about 40 percent on their benchmark. In health, a citation also tells the engine the claim did not come from nowhere.
  3. Limits. One plain sentence said what the app is not: not a diagnosis and not a replacement for a clinician. Teams often cut these lines as legal clutter. The engines appeared to read them as part of the description.
  4. Authorship. A named person was made responsible for the content and linked to a real profile, so the page had an accountable author behind it.

Each edit ran on its own, so a change in the answers could be traced to one variable. Suede does not publish percentages for these rounds, because prompt samples on small apps are too noisy to support them. What Jason Colapietro reports is the direction: scoped, sourced versions with stated limits were described more often and more accurately.

What looked like it should help and did not?

Publishing more pages did not help. Spinning out near-identical pages for every symptom is what Google's spam policies call scaled content abuse, and in health it carries extra risk because thin pages tend to make the broadest claims. Suede keeps its experiments on its own apps for this reason, and each test build is a working product. The note on GEO experiments that failed covers more of what did nothing.

User testimonials were another weak lever. A quoted user saying an app "changed my life" is a line an engine tends to skip in health, since it cannot check the line and repeating it would read as an endorsement.

What should a health app owner watch?

Watch how engines describe you as well as whether they name you. A health app named with a feature it lacks, or with a claim it never made, has a bigger problem than one left out. Keep the prompt set fixed, save each answer with a date, and compare the engine's description against your listing line by line. The note on measuring AI referrals honestly explains how to keep those records useful.

Treat the store listing as seriously as the website. Apple's App Store search guidance says results draw on matches with your app's title, subtitle, keywords and primary category, and AI engines read public listing pages too. Suede writes the first lines of a health listing so they would still be accurate if quoted alone.

How does this connect to money and relationship apps?

Engines apply a similar caution to money, where the risk is a wrong number, and handle relationship questions differently, where the risk is misreading the person asking. The wealth apps note and the relationship apps note cover those, and the pattern that held across all three ties them together.

Questions health app teams ask

Do disclaimers hurt a health app's chances of being recommended?

In Suede's tests, a short plain-language limit statement did not hurt, as long as it sat beside a clear description of what the app does. Long legal blocks made no visible difference either way.

Does a health app need medical credentials to be cited?

Suede's results do not show that credentials are required. Accountable authorship and sources an engine can check were among the edits that helped.
