After the Spike: What Suede Does to Keep Being Named by AI Assistants

By Jason Colapietro, Founder, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Getting named by ChatGPT takes a page an assistant can quote. Staying named takes maintenance, and much of that maintenance is leaving the cited page alone. When Suede Sing went from at most 5 users to more than 1,000 active users, combined across all its platforms, within about a week of ChatGPT naming it, the first instinct was to start changing things. The routine below starts by leaving the page alone.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, runs the same post-spike routine on every app in its lab. This note lays it out in the order it happens. The Suede Sing case study covers the spike itself.

Why is the first move to change nothing?

Whatever got the page cited is sitting in front of you, and you do not know which part of it mattered. Rewriting the headline, renaming the app or moving it to a new URL can each remove the thing the model was relying on. An assistant that fetches the page and finds a different description, or a redirect, has less reason to keep naming it.

So the cited URL stays where it is. The name stays the same, and so does the opening description. Jason Colapietro treats the cited version as a control. Changes come later, one at a time, where they can be measured against it.

What gets checked in the first days?

Access comes first. OpenAI lists the crawlers behind ChatGPT's search and browsing in its bots documentation: OAI-SearchBot for search, ChatGPT-User for fetches a user triggers, and GPTBot for training. Perplexity documents its own in its bots guide. Suede reads the live robots.txt, following Google's robots.txt introduction for syntax, and confirms none of the search crawlers is blocked. A traffic spike is also when someone is tempted to add rate limits or a firewall rule, and that rule can shut out the same bots that sent the traffic.

Next is availability. A recommendation that leads to an error page teaches users, and eventually the assistant, that the tool is unreliable. The page needs to load fast and work under the new load.

How do you make the facts agree everywhere?

Models build their description of a product from every source they can reach: the page, store listings, structured data, other people's mentions. When those disagree, the description gets vaguer or wrong. After a spike, Suede lines up the same one-sentence description and the same name across the app page, any store listing and the page's markup.

For markup, Suede uses schema.org SoftwareApplication (or WebApplication and MobileApplication where they fit), following Google's software app structured data guidance. The fields that matter most here are the plain ones: name, applicationCategory, operatingSystem, url and a description that matches the visible page. The note on structured data for apps goes further into the entity links.

What gets added, and what never does?

A cited page can gain substance without losing what it had. Jason Colapietro adds below the existing content: a short explanation of how the tool works, a checkable fact with its source, a dated changelog line. The GEO research from Aggarwal and colleagues (KDD 2024) found that citations, quotations and statistics raised visibility in generative answers by up to about 40 percent, and that matches what the lab has seen: specifics help.

What never gets added is a pile of near-copies. After a spike it is tempting to spin up a page for every phrasing of the query that worked. Google calls that pattern scaled content abuse in its spam policies, and it splits the signal the original page had built up.

How do you know if you are still being named?

You ask. Suede keeps a fixed list of the questions that produced the citation, in the user's words, and re-asks them on a schedule in ChatGPT and other assistants. Each run is saved with the date, the exact prompt, the assistant and a screenshot of the answer. Answers vary between runs, so a single miss means little. A pattern of misses across several runs is worth acting on.

Analytics run alongside. Active users and referral sources show whether the people are still arriving, and the prompt log shows whether the mention is still there. The two can disagree for a while, since users who found the tool keep coming back on their own. The note on measuring AI referrals covers how to keep those records straight.

When does Suede start changing things again?

Once the page is stable, accessible and consistent, experiments resume under the Suede iteration loop: one variable per round, recorded against the frozen version, with the prompt log as the check. If a change coincides with the app dropping out of answers, it gets rolled back. Jason Colapietro has run thousands of app iterations this way, and the cited page gets the same care as any other test.

Questions about keeping a citation

Should I rename my product after an AI assistant starts recommending it?

Not while the mention is fresh. A new name breaks the match between what the assistant says and what users find.

Does llms.txt help keep the citation?

The llms.txt proposal is cheap to add, but treat it as optional. Crawler access, a stable page and consistent facts come first.
