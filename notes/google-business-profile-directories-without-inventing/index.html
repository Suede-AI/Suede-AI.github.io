Staking Your Claim on a Google Business Profile and Directories Without Inventing Anything

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede AI · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 15, 2026

A Google Business Profile with a keyword-stuffed name, a borrowed street address and a rating nobody earned can rank for a few weeks. Then a competitor suggests an edit, a customer reports it, or Google's own checks catch up, and the listing that was supposed to be your foundation becomes the reason you disappear from the map.

Jason Colapietro wrote Stake Your Claim about ownership: own what you make, and make it something that lasts. A business listing is one of the few places on the internet where a small company can stake a claim in the literal sense, because Google calls the process claiming and verifying. This note covers how Suede AI approaches that claim, and the directory listings around it, for businesses in Jupiter, West Palm Beach and the rest of Palm Beach County. One rule sits above everything else: publish only what you can prove.

Why the profile matters more now that answers are generated

When someone asks Google Maps or an AI assistant for a mobile boat detailer near Jupiter Inlet, the answer is assembled from a handful of sources that agree with each other. The Business Profile is usually the anchor: name, category, service area, hours, website and reviews. Directories and your own website act as corroboration. When those sources agree, a model can state facts about you with confidence. When they conflict, it hedges, or it names a competitor whose facts line up.

That is why invented details hurt twice. A fake suite number or an inflated category can get the listing suspended, and it also spreads into every data aggregator and directory that copied it. Cleaning a fabricated address out of dozens of downstream listings takes longer than building the honest version would have taken in the first place.

The five fields worth getting exactly right

Most of a profile is optional. These five are not, and Jason Colapietro's team checks them first on every engagement.

  1. Business name. Use the name on your signage, invoices and state registration. Google's guidelines prohibit adding keywords or locations that are not part of the real name. "Coastal Detailing" stays "Coastal Detailing", not "Coastal Detailing Jupiter Boat Cleaning Best Prices".
  2. Address or service area. If customers come to you, list the address where they arrive. If you go to them, set the listing up as a service-area business, hide the street address, and name the towns you genuinely serve, such as Jupiter, Tequesta, Juno Beach and Palm Beach Gardens. A virtual mailbox or a coworking desk you never use is not a location, and treating it as one is a frequent cause of suspensions.
  3. Primary category. Pick the narrowest category that describes the core of the business, then add secondary categories only for work you do regularly. Categories describe the business you have, not the one you hope to have.
  4. Hours and phone. Real hours and a real number, answered by a person or a clearly labeled system. Update holiday hours before the holiday, not after the first missed call.
  5. Website link. Point it at a page that repeats the same name, service area and phone, ideally with Organization or LocalBusiness structured data carrying those same facts. Directories: fewer, better and consistent

The old local SEO advice was to submit to hundreds of citation sites. Most of them no longer matter to people, and a model gives a handful of trusted sources far more weight than a long tail of scraped ones. Suede works the other way: pick the directories a buyer in your category would actually check, claim each one, and make every field match the Business Profile character for character.

For a home services company in Palm Beach County that usually means Apple Business Connect, Bing Places, the Better Business Bureau if you are accredited, your trade association's member directory, and one or two platforms specific to your trade. For an agency or consultancy it means the review platforms buyers use to build a shortlist.

What "without inventing anything" rules out

A short list of things the Suede Labs team will not do on a client listing, however tempting the short-term bump looks:

  • Rent a virtual office to appear in a town where the business has no real presence.
  • Add city names or service keywords to the business name.
  • Buy, swap or gate reviews. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, in force since October 2024, prohibits fake reviews, undisclosed reviews from insiders, and incentives conditioned on a positive review.
  • Create duplicate profiles for each service line or each town.
  • List awards, licenses or "years in business" that cannot be linked to a source.

Each of those creates a fact that someone, human or machine, can falsify. A falsified fact costs you the credibility of every true one sitting next to it.

A working week for a Jupiter service business

Here is the order Jason Colapietro recommends when a business starts from a half-finished or unclaimed profile.

Monday: search your business name and phone number on Google, Apple Maps and Bing. Write down every listing you find, including duplicates and ones you never created.

Tuesday: claim or request access to the Google profile and complete verification. Fix the five fields above, and delete anything you cannot prove.

Wednesday: claim Apple Business Connect and Bing Places, copying the same name, phone, hours and service area exactly.

Thursday: update your website's contact page and footer to match, and add structured data with the same facts.

Friday: ask five recent customers, by name, for an honest review on the platform they already use, and reply to every review you already have. Note the date. In thirty days, ask three assistants about your business and see whether they describe it correctly.
