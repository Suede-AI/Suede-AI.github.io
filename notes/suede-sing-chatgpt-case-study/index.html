Suede Sing Case Study: From Under Five Users to 1,000+ in the Week ChatGPT Named It

By Jason Colapietro, Founder, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Suede Sing is a small web app: a vocal coach, a pitch trainer and a vocal range test on one page. For most of its life it had never had more than five users. Then ChatGPT started naming it when people asked for singing tools, and within about a week it passed 1,000 active users. Both numbers come from the app's own analytics, as reported by Jason Colapietro, who built it.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, has run a lot of experiments on its own apps. This is the cleanest result any of them has produced, and also the easiest to oversell. This note covers what happened, what the app looked like when an AI assistant picked it up, and what the jump proves.

What was Suede Sing before that week?

It was one of the more than 100 apps Jason Colapietro has built. More than 20 of them are listed in public stores, and many exist for one reason: to find out what search engines and generative engines respond to. He calls the method the Suede iteration loop. You change one thing in an app, its listing or its page, watch what moves, and carry the result forward. Across thousands of iterations in health, wealth and relationship apps, most rounds teach something small.

Sing was a single-purpose tool. You open it, it listens, and it tells you whether you hit the note and where your range sits. A handful of people had found it.

What changed?

ChatGPT began recommending it. OpenAI does not tell a site owner when its assistant starts naming them, so the first sign was the curve in the analytics. The user count went from a number you could count on one hand to four figures in roughly seven days.

No daily series is shown on purpose. The two reported numbers are the before state and where the count stood a week later. Drawing a smooth curve between them would mean making up the days in between.

Why could an assistant name it at all?

Nobody outside OpenAI can say exactly why one tool lands in an answer, and Jason Colapietro does not claim to know. His read, after watching many apps get picked up or ignored, is that Sing had a few things going for it.

  • The page says what the tool does in the words people ask with. Someone types "free vocal range test" or "app to check if I'm singing in tune", and the page describes exactly that.
  • The app does one job, so a model summarizing it has little room to get it wrong.
  • The URL is stable and public, and the tool runs in the browser, so a recommendation sends the user somewhere that does what the answer promised.
  • ChatGPT's search features fetch pages through crawlers OpenAI documents, including OAI-SearchBot and ChatGPT-User. Suede checks that its apps do not block them by accident in robots.txt.

None of that is exotic. The research points the same direction: the GEO paper from Aggarwal and colleagues (KDD 2024) found that adding citations, quotations and statistics to a source raised its visibility in generative answers by up to about 40 percent in their tests.

What does the jump prove, and what doesn't it?

It proves that a generative answer can move a small product from nearly zero to real usage fast.

It does not prove a formula. This is one app and one engine, with one before figure and one after figure. Suede did not run a controlled test where a twin of Sing was hidden from ChatGPT. The active-user figure is from Suede's analytics and measures people using the app, which differs from installs or store acquisition events. The note on Suede Voice's Google Play data explains why those two kinds of number should never be added together.

Jason Colapietro treats the Sing result as a strong signal to test against. If something about Sing's page helped, it should show up again when the same change is made to other apps one variable at a time. That is how the app lab turns a surprise into a rule that client work can use.

What should a small product owner take from it?

Start by writing your main page so an assistant could quote it without editing: what the product does, for whom, and what happens when someone opens it. Keep that description identical in your store listing, your site and your structured data. Make sure the crawlers that feed AI answers can reach the page. Then measure honestly, with a dated before figure, so you can tell a real change from noise.

A spike also brings its own problem: keeping the mention once you have it. Suede's routine for that is in After the spike: keeping the citation, and the reason one answer can outweigh a whole results page is in One ChatGPT answer beat page one.

Questions people ask about the Suede Sing jump

How fast did Suede Sing grow?

From a ceiling of five users to more than 1,000 active users within about a week of ChatGPT starting to name it, according to Suede's analytics as reported by Jason Colapietro.

Is 1,000 active users the same as 1,000 downloads?

No. Active users are people using the app, counted in Suede's own analytics. Downloads and store acquisitions are counted by the store and can include people who never open the app twice.

Can this be repeated for a client?

The mechanism can be tested; the size of the jump cannot be promised. Suede's client work at suedeai.agency uses what the app lab found, and the record behind it is on Jason Colapietro's founder page.
