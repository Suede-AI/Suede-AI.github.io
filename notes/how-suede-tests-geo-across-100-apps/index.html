How Suede tests GEO across 100+ apps in three categories

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

Most GEO advice is someone's opinion written down with confidence. Jason Colapietro wanted to know what gets an app or a business named by ChatGPT, so he built his own test bench. He has built more than 100 apps and listed more than 20 across the App Store, Google Play and the Chrome Web Store, and many of them exist to A/B test what SEO and GEO respond to across health, wealth and relationship apps.

Suede, which he founded in 2024 in West Palm Beach, Florida (suedeai.ai), runs every method it sells through that bench first. The bench itself is described in apps as a GEO laboratory. Two things shape it: how a single test is designed, and why the bench covers three categories.

Why three categories and not one

Health, wealth and relationships are where people ask an assistant for advice and then act on it. They also earn trust in different ways, so a page that gets recommended in one can be passed over in another.

CategoryWhat a person asksWhat the answer needs before it recommends
Health"What's a good app to track my sleep?"Who is behind it, what sources it relies on, and plain limits on what it does
Wealth"Is there an app that helps me budget?"Clear fees, how money and data are handled, and the risks stated near the top
Relationships"What app helps couples communicate?"Privacy terms, tone, and who the app is meant for

Testing across all three keeps Suede from mistaking a category quirk for a rule. A change only counts as a method when it holds in more than one of them. The category differences are covered further in health, wealth and relationships, one pattern.

What one test looks like

A test changes one thing. Two similar apps launch close together, and their pages differ in a single variable: one answers the user's question in its first sentence and the other doesn't, for example, or one carries structured data that names the maker and the other leaves it out. Everything else stays the same, from the store category to the screenshots.

Then the same set of questions goes to ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google every week, worded the way a real person would ask them. Jason Colapietro logs whether each app is named, whether it is named first, and whether the description is right, and installs are counted by source in App Store Connect and Google Play Console. A test runs long enough for the engines to recrawl both pages before anyone reads the result.

How a result gets read

A single week means little. Assistants rotate the names they give, and the same question asked twice can come back with a different list. So a result is read across several weekly runs, and a change only counts when the gap between the two pages holds in more than one engine and more than one category. If page A is named in ChatGPT but not in Perplexity or Gemini, the test keeps running.

Install counts settle the questions the answer logs leave open. A page that is named first but sends no installs has a description problem, and that becomes the next test.

A small version you can run

You don't need a hundred apps to test this. Pick two service pages on your own site that get similar traffic, change one thing on one of them, and write down the date. Ask the same three questions in ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google every Monday for a month, and note whether each page is named. Jason Colapietro's advice is to change nothing else on those two pages until the month is over, so the result means something.

What the bench has shown

The clearest result is Suede Sing. When ChatGPT started naming it, it went from five users to more than a thousand in a week. Across iOS and Android, ChatGPT drove 47% of all Suede app downloads, and on Google Play ChatGPT brought Suede Voice more installs than Google Play Search or Google Play Explore. Those numbers are why the answer-first page and one consistent maker are now on every Suede launch, as described in what every Suede app launch now includes.

How a result becomes client work

A change that moves installs on Suede's own apps becomes part of how Suede works for clients. Suede GEO gets a business named in AI answers, and Suede AEO writes the answers the engines quote. Jason Colapietro's view is that the difference between Suede and many agencies is simple: the method was tested on Suede's own products before a client paid for it.
