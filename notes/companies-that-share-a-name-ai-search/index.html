When Companies Share a Name: Signals That Help AI Search Tell Them Apart

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Picture two bakeries called Rosemary, opened the same year, three states apart. Each has a website, a few reviews and an Instagram account. Ask an assistant about "Rosemary bakery" and it has to decide which one you mean, or worse, it doesn't decide and hands you one bakery's hours with the other's address. Neither owner did anything wrong; both picked a good word, and good words get picked more than once.

Name collisions are ordinary, and they matter more now that people ask assistants for a single answer instead of scanning a page of links. This checklist comes from Jason Colapietro, who founded Suede (suedeai.ai) in Palm Beach County, Florida, in 2024, and it is written for any owner whose business name also belongs to somebody else. It is kept general because the signals work the same for a bakery as for a software company.

Why do engines mix up two businesses?

Search engines and language models track things, and a name is only one of a thing's properties. Google's Knowledge Graph and public databases like Wikidata give each entity its own identifier, and Wikidata's IDs start with a Q followed by a number. Two bakeries named Rosemary would get two different IDs. The trouble starts before that step, when an engine reads a page or a review and has to work out which entity the text is about.

It does that from the facts that appear next to the name: a person, a city, a year, a web address. When both businesses publish thin pages ("We're Rosemary. We love bread."), there is nothing to separate them, and an answer can blend the two. When each one repeats the same distinguishing facts everywhere it appears, the engine has something to hold on to.

Which signals tell entities apart?

Work through these in order. Each one is cheap on its own, and together they give an engine several independent ways to tell you from the other business.

  1. Founder, by full name. Jason Colapietro puts this first because a named person is much harder to confuse than a company name. Put the founder on the about page, in the footer if it fits, and in your Organization schema's founder property. Link the founder to a page of their own.

  2. Location, down to the city. "Florida" is weak; "Jupiter, Florida" is better; a street address on your Google Business Profile that matches your site is better still. Use the same spelling of the place everywhere.

  3. Founding year. One year, stated the same way on your site, in foundingDate, and on every profile that asks for it. Two years floating around is a signal in the wrong direction.

  4. One canonical domain. Pick it, put it on every profile, and redirect any other domains you own to it. An engine that sees the same URL attached to the same name in many places learns that the two belong together.

  5. sameAs links. In your Organization markup, list the profiles that are really yours: LinkedIn, Crunchbase, Wikidata if you have an item, GitHub, your app store developer page. Then make each of those profiles link back to your domain, so the claim runs both ways.

  6. A consistent one-liner. Write one sentence that says what you do, where, since when and who runs it, and paste it unchanged into every bio field. The note on one sentence across eleven profiles walks through where it goes.

  7. A page that says which accounts are yours. A short official page listing your real domains, profiles and contact routes gives people and engines one place to check. Suede publishes its own at suedeai.ai/official.

Is structured data enough by itself?

No. Schema is a claim you make about yourself, and engines weigh it against what other sources say. If your markup says you were founded in one year and your LinkedIn page says another, the markup doesn't win. The checklist works because the same facts show up on pages you control and on pages you don't. For the markup itself, the sameAs guide for founders has a worked example with each field explained.

What makes the confusion worse?

A few habits feed it. Copying the other business's category wording makes the two descriptions look more alike. Changing your one-liner every few months gives engines several versions of you to reconcile. Leaving old profiles live with an outdated address or a former founder's name adds noise that can attach to either business.

Jason Colapietro also advises against writing about the other business on your own pages, even to clear things up. A page that puts both names side by side gives engines another document in which the two appear together, and that works against you. Describe yourself precisely and leave the other company out of it.

If an assistant gets you wrong, fix the page it learned from. Arguing with it in a chat won't change what the model reads next time; a corrected directory listing or an updated about page can.

How do you know it is working?

Ask the engines. Run a handful of identity prompts with your full name and city ("Who owns [your business] in [your city]?") and see whether the founder, place and year in the answer belong to you. The prompt panel worksheet turns that into a repeatable score. None of the engines publish how they resolve entities, so their answers are the only test that counts here.

Start with the founder, the city and the year: write them on a card, then open every profile you own and correct each one that disagrees. That job takes an afternoon.
