What Shipping AI Features Across 20+ Listed Apps Taught Suede About Deployment Engineering

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

An AI feature in a store-listed app has two release schedules. The app binary moves at the speed of store review. The model behind the feature moves whenever its provider decides to ship something, and nobody sends you a changelog that says your app now answers differently. Most deployment trouble in AI apps comes from treating those two schedules as one.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, has shipped AI features across many of the apps Jason Colapietro has built. He has built more than 100, and over 20 are listed in public stores, including the iOS titles on his Apple developer page and Suede Voice on Google Play. Many of those apps existed to test what search and AI engines respond to, which is covered in why Suede built apps as a GEO lab. This note covers the engineering underneath: what it took to keep AI features working, and testable, once they were in front of real users.

Why does the prompt live on a server and not in the app?

A prompt stored inside the app is fine until it needs fixing. Then a one-line wording change means a new build, a new review and an unknown wait, and every installed copy keeps sending the old prompt until its owner gets around to updating.

Suede moved prompts, model choices and the switches that control AI features to a small server it controls. The app asks the server what to do and the server calls the model. The rule Jason Colapietro applies is simple: anything you might need to change on a Tuesday afternoon cannot live in a file that needs store approval. The binary handles screens and audio, and the behavior that changes often stays off the device.

Is the model a dependency or a release?

Treat it as a release. Model providers update the models behind familiar names, and an alias that pointed to one model last month may point to a newer one today. Output length, tone and refusals can all shift. For a coaching app that tells someone how their singing went, a shift in tone is a product change.

So Suede pins exact model versions where the provider allows it, logs the model identifier next to every response, and moves to a new version on purpose, the same way it would ship a new screen. When an answer looks wrong, the log says which model wrote it. Without that line, you end up arguing about whether the app changed or the model did.

What happens when the model call fails?

It will fail: timeouts, rate limits, an outage at the provider, a phone on a bad connection. A spinner that never ends is the worst response, because the user cannot tell whether to wait.

Every AI feature Suede ships now has a defined fallback. Where the core result can be computed without a model, the app computes it locally and the model only adds explanation on top. Where it cannot, the app says the feature is unavailable and offers the non-AI path. Short timeouts matter more than clever retries, because users put up with a plain error message far better than a frozen screen.

Does the store listing have to change when the AI feature changes?

Yes, and this is the lesson that tied deployment to GEO. A store listing is a public description of what the app does. Apple's App Store search guidance treats the name, subtitle and keywords as what search matches against. AI engines read listings too, as store listings as citation sources explains. When an AI feature is added, renamed or removed and the listing is not updated, users read one thing and find another, and so do engines.

Suede added the listing to the release checklist. A change to what the AI feature does triggers a review of the description, the screenshots and the structured data on the app's web page, using the fields in Google's software app structured data documentation. Suede ships the listing update and the behavior change together.

How do you test AI changes without breaking the app?

With switches. Every AI feature sits behind a flag on the server, which lets Suede change one thing for one app, watch, and switch it back. That is the deployment half of the Suede iteration loop: one variable per round, with a dated record of what changed.

On the store side, Apple's product page optimization and Google Play's store listing experiments split real traffic between listing variants. Suede runs those for the human side and keeps the AI behavior constant while a listing test runs, so any difference belongs to the listing.

What Jason Colapietro would tell a team shipping its first AI feature

  • Keep prompts and model choice on a server, and log the exact model version beside every output.
  • Decide what the app shows when the model fails before you decide what it shows when it works.
  • Put the store listing and the web page that describes the app on the same checklist as the code.
  • Put every AI behavior behind a switch so you can change one thing and take it back.

This plumbing is what let Suede run thousands of app iterations without losing track of which change did what. The integration side, making the product something an engine can read and cite, is covered in AI integration engineering for GEO.

Questions people ask

Do I need my own server for one AI feature? If the feature's prompt might change after launch, yes. A small function behind an API key you hold is enough.

Should the fallback be another model? Sometimes. Suede prefers a fallback that does not depend on any model provider, because outages and rate limits tend to arrive together.
