Health, Wealth and Relationships: The One GEO Pattern That Held Across All Three

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

Health, money and relationship apps look like three separate GEO problems. Health brings medical caution, money brings numbers and disclosures, and relationship questions are long and emotional. Suede tested them separately and expected to end up with separate playbooks. The results kept pointing at one rule: an AI engine names the app whose own sentences it can repeat to a stranger without taking a risk.

Suede is the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida. Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps, 17 of them live in public stores (9 App Store, 6 Google Play, 2 Chrome extensions), and many exist only to test what search and generative engines respond to. Those tests ran over thousands of iterations in health, wealth and relationship apps. This note is about what the three sets of rounds had in common.

What is the pattern?

Inside Suede it is called repeatability. An engine that quotes your sentence in an answer is lending its own credibility to it. It does that more readily when the sentence is specific enough to check, sourced or scoped so it is unlikely to be wrong, and consistent with everything else the engine can read about you. When one of those was missing, engines in Suede's tests tended to hedge, paraphrase loosely or name a different app.

The GEO study by Aggarwal and colleagues (KDD 2024) points the same way from a different starting place. Edits that added citations, quotations and statistics to source content raised visibility in generative answers by up to about 40 percent on their benchmark, while keyword stuffing did poorly. Each of the edits that helped makes a passage easier to verify.

How did the pattern show up in each vertical?

The risk the engine is trying to avoid differs by vertical, so the fix looks different on the surface.

For health apps the risk is harm. Listings became safe to repeat when claims were scoped to what the app actually does, sources were named and a plain sentence stated the limits.

Money is different because the danger is a wrong number. In the wealth app rounds, exact and dated prices and disclosures helped most when the store listing, the website and the structured data all said the same thing.

Relationship questions carry the risk of misreading the person who asked. There the relationship app tests favored pages written around the situations people describe, in their own phrasing, with a plain statement of what the app does.

Why did Suede expect three playbooks?

Classic SEO advice is usually split by vertical. Medical sites get expertise checklists, finance gets compliance reviews, and relationship content gets long-tail keyword research. Google's guidance on helpful, people-first content does hold health and finance to a higher bar for trust as "Your Money or Your Life" topics. Jason Colapietro assumed the generative engines would add vertical quirks of their own. The Suede iteration loop found those quirks, but they were small next to the shared rule.

Where does the Suede Sing story fit?

Suede Sing is a vocal coach, pitch trainer and vocal range test, so it sits outside all three verticals. Its listing was written to the same rule, with what the app does stated in sentences that can be quoted alone. Suede Sing went from at most 5 users to more than 1,000 active users, combined across all its platforms, within about a week of ChatGPT naming it. The Suede Sing case study covers that week. It is the clearest example Suede has of what follows when an engine decides a product is safe to name.

What does this mean for client work?

Suede runs experiments on its own apps so that client sites never serve as the test bed, a choice explained in why Suede built an app lab. Each test app is a working product. Mass-producing pages was never the method, and Google's spam policies treat that as scaled content abuse. For a client, the pattern becomes a question asked of every important sentence on the site: could an engine repeat this to a stranger without checking with you first?

Most of the fixes are editing work. Replace adjectives with facts, put sources next to claims, and make the store listing, the website and the JSON-LD agree. Where people ask in stories, write sections around the situations they describe.

Questions about the pattern

Is this pattern specific to ChatGPT?

Suede ran its prompt sets across several engines, and the pattern held broadly. Engines differ in which sources they prefer, so test each engine you care about.

Does it apply outside health, wealth and relationships?

Suede Sing suggests it does, though the evidence outside the three verticals is thinner. Treat it as a strong default and test it on your own product.
