Organization Schema for Founders: @id, founder and sameAs, Field by Field

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

Below is a complete Organization block for a made-up company, Example Co, followed by what each field does for a search engine trying to work out who you are.

{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Organization",
  "@id": "https://example.com/#organization",
  "name": "Example Co",
  "alternateName": ["Example Company", "ExampleCo"],
  "url": "https://example.com/",
  "logo": "https://example.com/logo.png",
  "description": "Example Co builds scheduling software for dental practices. Founded 2021 by Jane Doe in Jupiter, Florida.",
  "founder": { "@id": "https://example.com/about/jane-doe#person" },
  "foundingDate": "2021-03",
  "foundingLocation": {
    "@type": "Place",
    "name": "Jupiter, Florida",
    "address": {
      "@type": "PostalAddress",
      "addressLocality": "Jupiter",
      "addressRegion": "FL",
      "addressCountry": "US"
    }
  },
  "sameAs": [
    "https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q00000000",
    "https://www.linkedin.com/company/example-co",
    "https://www.crunchbase.com/organization/example-co",
    "https://github.com/example-co",
    "https://apps.apple.com/us/developer/example-co/id000000000"
  ]
}

Every value above is a placeholder. Jane Doe, the dates and the IDs are invented for the example, and the Wikidata ID in particular should be your own item's ID or left out.

Why founders should care about this block

A founder-led company usually has two entities that search engines need to connect: the company and the person who started it. Suede is one example: Jason Colapietro founded it in 2024 in Palm Beach County, Florida (suedeai.ai), and an engine answering a question about either one has to work out that the two belong together. Organization markup restates what your pages and profiles already say, in a form a parser can read without guessing, and ties those facts to one stable identifier. If the pages and profiles say something different, the markup will not override them.

Field by field

@id
A URL that names the entity, separate from any page. Pick one, usually your homepage plus #organization, and use the exact same string on every page that mentions the company. Other nodes, such as an Article's publisher, point back to it by that string.
name and alternateName
The name is what you call yourself on the homepage. alternateName holds spellings people actually use: an older name, a shortened form, a version without a space. Keep it to real variants.
url and logo
The canonical homepage and a logo file that stays at the same address. Google reads the logo property for Organization markup.
description
One sentence that matches the description on your homepage and your profiles word for word. The note on using one sentence across eleven profiles explains why the repetition helps.
founder
A reference by @id to a Person node, ideally defined once on an about or founder page with its own name, jobTitle and sameAs. A plain string such as "Jane Doe" gives a parser a name and nothing to connect it to. The Person node can carry its own sameAs list too; Jason Colapietro's iOS apps, for example, ship under his own Apple developer account, which is the kind of page that list is for.
foundingDate
An ISO 8601 date: a year, a year and month, or a full date. "Spring 2021" is text a machine has to interpret.
foundingLocation
A Place with a name and a PostalAddress. For a company that shares its name with others, location is one of the cheapest distinguishing facts you have.
sameAs
URLs of profiles that describe this same organization: your Wikidata item, LinkedIn company page, Crunchbase, GitHub, app store developer page. Each one is a claim that says "that page is about us."

Mistakes that split one company into two

  • A different @id on different pages, such as /#org on the homepage and /about#company on the about page. A parser sees two identifiers and has no instruction to merge them.
  • sameAs pointing at a news article, a search results page or a directory category. Those pages mention you, and sameAs is meant for pages whose subject is you.
  • sameAs pointing at a profile of a different company with the same name. This happens when someone copies a Wikidata ID from a search without checking the founder and location on the item. The guide to companies that share a name covers the other signals that keep two entities apart.
  • Profiles that do not link back. A sameAs URL is stronger when the profile itself lists your website.
  • Keyword stuffing in alternateName. "Example Co best dental scheduling Florida" is not a name anyone uses.
  • Markup that disagrees with the visible page, for example a founding year in JSON-LD that differs from the year on the about page.

It helps to keep the facts in one public place that every profile and every block of markup can be checked against. For Suede, that is the official Suede page, which Jason Colapietro publishes as the company's verification record. The profile of who Suede is shows those facts in plain language.

Check it before you ship it

Paste your block into the Schema Markup Validator at validator.schema.org and fix every error it reports, then run the page through Google's Rich Results Test. Next, open each sameAs URL in a private window and confirm three things on the profile: the same name, the same founding facts, and a link back to your site. Any profile that fails one of those checks comes out of the list until it is corrected.
