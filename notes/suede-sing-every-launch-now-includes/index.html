The ChatGPT app launch checklist Suede has run since Suede Sing

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

At most 5 users. About a week later, more than 1,000, combined across all its platforms. Nothing changed except that ChatGPT started recommending the app. That app was Suede Sing, the online singing-practice tool from Suede, the company Jason Colapietro founded in 2024 in West Palm Beach, Florida (suedeai.ai). The Suede Sing case study tells that week from the start. What came after is the launch list Jason Colapietro now runs on every Suede app, so the next one is ready to be named on day one: an answer page, one maker everywhere, crawler access, tagged links and a weekly check. The same five steps apply to any business that wants to be the name an assistant gives.

Want to know whether ChatGPT can name your business today? A free Suede Scan shows what the AI assistants say about you right now.

Across iOS and Android, ChatGPT drove 47% of all Suede app downloads, so the effect goes well past one app. Google Play attributed 145 of 528 Suede Voice acquisition events (27.5%) to ChatGPT, more than it attributed to Google Play Search (72) or Google Play Explore (99). When an assistant is sending close to half of your installs, the launch has to be built for it with the same care that goes into the App Store listing.

1. A page that answers the question a person asks an assistant

People ask ChatGPT for a good app to practice singing, or a way to find their vocal range, and they don't ask for "Suede Sing" by name. So every Suede app gets a web page whose first lines answer that question plainly: what the app does, who it is for, what it costs, and what makes it different. For Suede Sing that means saying up front that you hear a note, sing it back and see where your voice lands, with no install, no ads, and audio that stays on your device.

Those first lines are the sentence most likely to end up in an answer. Jason Colapietro writes them before anything else on the page, and the rest of the page backs them up. Any business can use the same habit, and the note on answer-first paragraphs walks through it.

2. One name and one maker on every listing

An assistant has to be sure which app it is recommending and who made it. Every Suede app carries the same name, the same maker and the same short description on the web page, the App Store listing, the Google Play listing and the page's structured data. Jason Colapietro's iOS apps all ship under his own Apple developer account, so the maker on the store matches the maker on the site.

When those records agree, an engine can treat them as one thing. If they disagree, it hedges or picks a different app, which is why how to keep one description across eleven profiles applies the same rule to a company's own listings.

3. Pages the crawlers can read

Before launch, each page is checked from the outside: the AI crawlers are allowed in robots.txt, the important text is in the HTML rather than painted in by a script after load, and the page is in the sitemap. If an assistant can't fetch a page, it has nothing to quote, however good the page is. Suede Scan runs this kind of check on any site.

4. Links tagged so installs can be traced

Every link Suede controls carries a source tag, and installs are tracked by source in App Store Connect and Google Play Console. That tracking is where Suede's figure comes from: ChatGPT drove 47% of its app downloads across iOS and Android. On Google Play, ChatGPT shows up by name as a source for Suede Voice; the Suede Voice case study has the numbers behind that.

5. A check every Monday

After launch, the same questions go to Google, the AI Overview, ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini every week. The log records whether the app is named, whether it is named first, and whether the description is right. Anything that slips gets fixed that week, following the routine written up in the weekly brand watch, the same check Suede uses in its GEO tests across 100+ apps.

Why does the checklist run in that order?

The order follows how an assistant gets to a recommendation. The answer page comes first because the assistant needs a sentence worth repeating, and the matching listings come next because it has to be sure who made the app. The crawler check covers whether it can fetch the page at all. Tagged links matter only after that, since they measure traffic the first three steps earn. When an app drops out of an answer, the Monday check shows which step slipped, and Jason Colapietro fixes that step before touching anything else.

The common mistake is skipping ahead. Tagging links on an app no assistant can read measures nothing, and a perfect answer page on a site that blocks crawlers never gets seen.

The same list, for your business

Every step on this list applies outside apps too. A dental practice in Jupiter or a law firm in West Palm Beach has the same five jobs: a page that answers the buyer's question, one consistent identity, pages crawlers can read, traffic you can trace, and a weekly look at what the assistants say. Jason Colapietro and the team at Suede's SEO, GEO and web practice run this list for clients, starting from what worked on Suede's own apps.
