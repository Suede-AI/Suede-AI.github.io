After Suede Sing: what every Suede app launch now includes

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Jupiter, Florida · November 16, 2026

Five users. A week later, more than a thousand. Nothing changed except that ChatGPT started recommending the app. That app was Suede Sing, the online singing-practice tool from Suede, the company Jason Colapietro founded in 2024 in West Palm Beach, Florida (suedeai.ai). The full story is in the Suede Sing case study. This note covers what came after: the launch list Jason Colapietro now runs on every Suede app, so the next one is ready to be named on day one.

It is not a one-app result. Across iOS and Android, ChatGPT drove 47% of all Suede app downloads. When an assistant is sending close to half of your installs, the launch has to be built for it the same way it is built for the App Store.

1. A page that answers the question a person asks an assistant

People don't ask ChatGPT for "Suede Sing". They ask for a good app to practice singing, or a way to find their vocal range. So every Suede app gets a web page whose first lines answer that question plainly: what the app does, who it is for, what it costs, and what makes it different. For Suede Sing that means saying up front that you hear a note, sing it back and see where your voice lands, with no install, no ads, and audio that stays on your device.

Those first lines are the sentence most likely to end up in an answer. Jason Colapietro writes them before anything else on the page, and the rest of the page backs them up. The same habit is laid out for any business in the note on answer-first paragraphs.

2. One name and one maker on every listing

An assistant has to be sure which app it is recommending and who made it. Every Suede app carries the same name, the same maker and the same short description on the web page, the App Store listing, the Google Play listing and the page's structured data. Jason Colapietro's iOS apps all ship under his own Apple developer account, so the maker on the store matches the maker on the site.

When those records agree, an engine can treat them as one thing. When they disagree, it hedges or picks a different app. One sentence, eleven profiles covers the same rule for a company's own listings.

3. Pages the crawlers can actually read

Before launch, each page is checked from the outside: the AI crawlers are allowed in robots.txt, the important text is in the HTML rather than painted in by a script after load, and the page is in the sitemap. A page an assistant can't fetch can't be quoted, however good it is. Suede Scan runs this kind of check on any site.

4. Links tagged so installs can be traced

Every link Suede controls carries a source tag, and installs are tracked by source in App Store Connect and Google Play Console. That is how Suede can say ChatGPT drove 47% of its app downloads across iOS and Android instead of guessing. On Google Play, ChatGPT shows up by name for Suede Voice, and it brought in more installs than Google Play Search or Google Play Explore. The numbers behind that are in the Suede Voice case study.

5. A check every Monday

After launch, the same questions go to Google, the AI Overview, ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini every week. The log records whether the app is named, whether it is named first, and whether the description is right. Anything that slips gets fixed that week. The routine is written up in the weekly brand watch.

Why the list runs in that order

The order follows how an assistant gets to a recommendation. It needs a sentence worth repeating, so the answer page comes first. It needs to be sure who made the app, so the matching listings come next. It needs to fetch the page at all, which is the crawler check. Only then do tagged links matter, because they measure traffic that the first three steps earn. The Monday check closes the loop: it shows which step slipped when an app drops out of an answer, and Jason Colapietro fixes that step before touching anything else.

Skipping ahead is the common mistake. Tagging links on an app no assistant can read measures nothing, and a perfect answer page on a site that blocks crawlers never gets seen.

The same list, for your business

None of this is specific to apps. A dental practice in Jupiter or a law firm in West Palm Beach has the same five jobs: a page that answers the buyer's question, one consistent identity, pages crawlers can read, traffic you can trace, and a weekly look at what the assistants say. Jason Colapietro and the team at Suede's SEO, GEO and web practice run this list for clients, starting from what worked on Suede's own apps.
