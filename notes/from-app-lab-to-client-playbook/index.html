From App Lab to Client Playbook: How Suede Decides Which App Findings Reach Client Work

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

A finding from one app is an anecdote. A finding that held across apps in different categories, survived a reversal test and still made sense on a website is something you can charge a client for. Most of the work between an app lab and a client practice is that filtering, and most findings do not make it through.

Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, runs two things that look separate from outside. One is the lab: more than 100 apps Jason Colapietro has built, over 20 of them listed in public stores, many created purely to test what search engines and AI engines respond to. Why Suede built apps as a GEO lab explains that choice. The other is Suede's SEO, GEO and web practice for clients. This note describes how material moves from the first to the second.

Check one: did it repeat in more than one vertical?

The lab spans health, wealth and relationship apps on purpose. Those categories attract different questions and different caution from engines. A change that helped a health app might have helped because engines are careful with medical topics, which says nothing about a law firm. Suede wants to see the same effect in at least two unrelated categories before treating it as general. The pattern that held across all three is in health, wealth and relationships: one pattern.

Check two: did it survive a reversal?

AI answers drift on their own. If a change seemed to work, Suede removes it in a later round of the Suede iteration loop and checks whether the result goes away. A result that stays after the change is gone was probably not caused by the change. Many tactics failed here, including some that looked convincing for a round or two. GEO experiments that failed lists them without dressing them up.

Check three: does it map to something the client owns?

An app has a store listing, a product page and a developer profile. A dental practice in West Palm Beach has a website, a Google Business Profile, directory listings and reviews. Some findings translate cleanly. The lesson that store listings work as citation sources became, for clients, a rule that third-party profiles should describe the business in plain sentences an engine can repeat. The lesson about entity links, from structured data for apps, carried over to Organization and LocalBusiness markup that matches the visible page.

Other findings have no client equivalent. Apple's product page optimization lets an app owner split real store traffic between listing variants. A local service business has nothing like that on its Google profile. Findings that depend on store mechanics stay in the lab.

What a client actually receives

The first thing is a baseline. Suede writes a fixed set of prompts a real customer would ask, runs them in the engines that matter for that business, and records who gets named and which sources are cited. Then come the access checks: whether robots rules or a firewall block the search crawlers OpenAI and Perplexity document publicly, and whether the pages render without scripts.

After that, changes go in one at a time, starting with the ones that passed all three checks most often in the lab. Typical early changes are adding a source behind a claim, rewriting a service description as specific statements, and fixing how the business is named across profiles. Those follow the direction of the GEO paper by Aggarwal and colleagues, where adding citations, quotations and statistics raised visibility in generative answers by up to about 40 percent on their benchmark.

Jason Colapietro draws a firm line here. The lab tells Suede what to try first. Each client's market, competitors and history get their own test, and Suede shows the client the same dated before-and-after captures it uses internally.

What never transfers

The lab could run thousands of app iterations because those apps were Suede's to test on. A client site does not get mass-produced pages, rewritten in bulk to see what sticks. Google's spam policies treat that as scaled content abuse, and Suede built the lab precisely so client sites would never carry that risk. What moves from lab to client is a short list of tested changes and a method for checking them, written to Google's standard for helpful, people-first content.

Questions people ask

Do clients see the lab data? They see the method and the findings that apply to them. The numbers Suede publishes, such as Suede Sing passing 1,000 active users within about a week after ChatGPT began naming it, come from the analytics of Suede's own apps.
