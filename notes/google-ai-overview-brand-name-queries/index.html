How a Google AI Overview Answers a Bare Brand-Name Query

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · November 16, 2026

Type the single word "suede" into Google and the engine has a decision to make before it writes anything. The person might want the napped leather on a pair of boots, a cleaning tip for a jacket, or a company. A bare brand-name query has no verb, no city and no stated intent, so whatever an AI Overview says comes from what Google already holds about the word. A business can shape only the part of that it publishes.

Suede, the AI company Jason Colapietro started in Palm Beach County, Florida, in 2024 (suedeai.ai), deals with this in practice, because its name is also a common noun. Google has described part of the path from query to overview in public, and a brand can change the outcome at several points along it.

Google first works out what the word refers to

Google describes AI Overviews as a Gemini model customized for Search, working with its ranking systems and its stores of facts such as the Knowledge Graph, and showing links to the pages behind the answer. It also says overviews appear only when its systems judge them helpful. Plenty of brand queries get ordinary blue links and a knowledge panel, with no generated summary at all.

When a word has a dictionary sense, that sense is the default. A company competes for the query with evidence: a site whose name matches the query, an entity record with a founder and a founding date, and other sites that describe the same business in the same terms. The weaker that evidence, the more likely the overview talks about leather. Jason Colapietro treats that as the starting position for Suede: the noun had a long head start, so the company has to be the better-documented meaning of the word.

The answer draws on four kinds of source

Google does not publish its full recipe, but the links it shows under overviews fall into recognisable groups.

The brand's own site
Homepage title, meta description, the opening paragraph, and any Organization markup. This is the only source the brand writes alone.
Profiles the brand fills in elsewhere
Google Business Profile, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, app store listings, Wikidata. The brand writes the text; another site hosts it and sets the field limits.
Pages other people write
Press coverage, directories, reviews and forum threads. These carry the most independent weight and are the hardest to correct.
Google's own entity records
The Knowledge Graph entry and the knowledge panel built from it, which Google assembles from the three groups above and from licensed data.

The homepage title carries more weight than most owners expect

The title tag is usually the first string Google shows for a site and the label a model sees attached to the page. A title such as "Home" or a slogan gives the engine nothing to restate. Say a hypothetical pool-service company in West Palm Beach called Tidewell titles its homepage "Welcome to Tidewell". An overview has to look elsewhere to learn what Tidewell does. If the title reads "Tidewell: pool cleaning and repair in West Palm Beach", the page names the business, its trade and its town before anyone opens it.

Jason Colapietro's advice to owners is to read the title as if it were the only line an assistant will ever see about them, because for a one-word query it sometimes is.

A repeated one-liner gives the model one phrasing to agree with

When the homepage, the LinkedIn tagline, the app listing and the Wikidata description use nearly the same words, a generated summary finds a single description backed by several pages that Google treats as separate sources. When each profile says something different, the model blends them, and blended descriptions drift until the founding year or the city no longer matches the company's own records.

One sentence, eleven profiles walks through writing one sentence and pasting it into every profile, along with the field limits that force a shorter version on some sites. Why Suede is just Suede covers the name and the company behind it, with its identity facts in one place.

Structured data ties the scattered pages to one entity

Organization markup with a stable @id, a founder, a founding date and a sameAs list tells crawlers which profiles belong together. It does not guarantee anything in an overview, and Google says structured data is not required for AI features, but any system that reads it has less to guess. The field-by-field version is in Organization schema and sameAs for founders.

Suede also keeps a plain official verification page listing the domains and profiles that belong to the company, written for people and readable by crawlers. Jason Colapietro links it from the places where a reader would want to confirm they have the right business.

A short check shows where your brand stands this week

Search your bare brand name today and save a screenshot of whatever Google returns, overview or not. Write down the sources it links and the first sentence it uses to describe you. Then view your homepage source, find the title tag, and set it beside that sentence. If they disagree, rewrite the title first, since it is the one source you control outright.
