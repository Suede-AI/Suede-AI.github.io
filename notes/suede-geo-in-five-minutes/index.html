Suede GEO in five minutes: get named by ChatGPT and Gemini

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

When someone asks ChatGPT who to hire, they get two or three names. There is no page two. Generative engine optimization, or GEO, is the work of being one of those names, described the right way, with a link the reader can follow. This is the five-minute version of how Suede does it, from Jason Colapietro, who founded Suede in 2024 in West Palm Beach, Florida (suedeai.ai).

Minute one: the answer is the new first page

Search used to hand a buyer ten links and let them choose. An assistant chooses for them. If your business isn't in the two or three names it gives, the buyer may never visit your site, and you won't see them in your analytics either. Across iOS and Android, ChatGPT drove 47% of all Suede app downloads, which is the clearest sign Jason Colapietro has that these answers send real customers.

Picture a homeowner in Palm Beach Gardens typing "who's a good roofer near me that does storm repairs?" The assistant reads a handful of sources, decides which businesses it trusts, and writes a short answer with two or three names and a sentence about each. The homeowner calls one of them. GEO is about everything that happens before that answer is written: which pages the assistant could read, whether it was sure who each business was, and whether it found a sentence it could repeat.

Minutes two and three: three layers

GEO works in three layers, and every problem sits in one of them.

Retrieval
Can ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google reach your pages? The AI crawlers have to be allowed in robots.txt, your main text has to be in the HTML, and your pages have to be in a sitemap.
Entity
Do they know who you are? One name, one founder, one location and one website, the same on your site, your Google Business Profile, LinkedIn, Wikidata and any app store. When those records agree, an engine treats them as one business.
Answer
Is there a sentence they can quote? The first lines under a question on your page should answer it plainly, the way you'd answer a customer on the phone.

If an assistant leaves you out, check retrieval first, then entity, then answer. The longer walk through each layer is in Suede GEO, layer by layer, and the answer layer gets its own treatment in answer-first paragraphs.

The three layers on one local business

Say a pool service in Jupiter asks ChatGPT who services pools nearby and doesn't appear. Retrieval comes first: its site blocks one of the AI crawlers in robots.txt, so that engine has never read a page of it. With that fixed, the entity layer shows the next gap: the business is "Blue Pool Co." on its site and "Blue Pool Company LLC" on its Google profile, with two different phone numbers. Once those match, the answer layer is what's left. Its services page opens with a paragraph about the owner's history instead of saying which towns it covers and what a weekly visit includes. Rewriting those first lines is the last step.

That order saves time. Rewriting the services page first would have done nothing while the crawler was blocked.

Four mistakes Jason Colapietro sees most

  1. Writing more content before checking that the engines can read the content already there.
  2. Using a slightly different business name on every profile.
  3. Burying the answer under a long introduction.
  4. Checking ChatGPT once, liking the answer, and never checking again.

Minute four: measure it every week

GEO you can't measure is a guess. Every Monday, Jason Colapietro asks the same questions in Google, the AI Overview, ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini, and logs three things for each one: is Suede named, is it named first, and is the description right. If something drops, it gets fixed that week. You can run the same routine with a spreadsheet; the scoring worksheet shows how to set it up, and the weekly brand watch shows Suede's own log.
