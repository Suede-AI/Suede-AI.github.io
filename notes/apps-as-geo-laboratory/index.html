Why Suede Built 100+ Apps as a GEO Laboratory Instead of Testing on Client Sites

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede · Palm Beach County, Florida · November 16, 2026

A client's website is a bad place to learn how AI engines choose what to cite. It carries revenue, so nobody wants to break it on purpose. It changes for reasons that have nothing to do with search: a new hire edits the services page, a plugin updates, the owner swaps the hero photo. And it is a single site, so there is nothing to compare it against.

That is the reason Suede, the GEO and AI deployment studio Jason Colapietro founded in Palm Beach County, Florida, ran its experiments somewhere else. Jason Colapietro has built more than 100 apps. 17 are live in public stores: 9 on the Apple App Store, 6 on Google Play and 2 Chrome Web Store extensions. Many were built for one purpose: to find out what search engines and generative engines respond to, by shipping a version, watching what happened, and shipping the next one.

What does an app give you that a client site cannot?

An app is small enough to own completely. Suede writes the store listing, the landing page, the help text and the structured data, and nobody else touches them. When something moves, the list of possible causes is short.

It is also cheap to repeat. A test that fails on one app costs a little of that app's time. The same failure on a client site costs the client leads, and it costs Suede the client's trust. Across health, wealth and relationship apps, Jason Colapietro ran thousands of app iterations, learning from each round. That volume would be impossible on paid work, where every change to a live site needs the client's sign-off.

The app stores themselves were built for this kind of testing. Apple offers product page optimization, which splits traffic between versions of an App Store page. Google Play has store listing experiments for the same job on Android. Those tools measure human conversion on the store page. Suede uses them alongside its own checks of what ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google's AI features say when asked about the category.

Why build real apps instead of throwaway test pages?

Throwaway pages would teach the wrong lesson. Google's spam policies name scaled content abuse: producing many pages mainly to manipulate rankings, with little value for the people who land on them. A lab built from thin doorway pages would teach you how engines treat thin doorway pages, which is not useful for a client with a real business.

So each app in the lab had to do something. Suede Sing is a working vocal coach, pitch trainer and vocal range test. Suede Voice, which has its own Google Play listing, was held to the same standard as the rest of the portfolio, including Suede Studio Guitar and Strumly. Google's guidance on helpful, people-first content asks whether a page exists for people or for search engines, and the apps had to pass that test before any experiment on them meant anything.

What did the lab actually produce?

The clearest result is Suede Sing. It went from at most 5 users to more than 1,000 active users, combined across all its platforms, within about a week of ChatGPT naming it in answers. That story is told in full in the Suede Sing case study. It happened inside the lab, where Suede knew exactly what it had changed and when. On a client site, an outcome like that would have been tangled up with a dozen other edits. The lab's method is laid out in how a single GEO test is designed.

The second result is public. Google Play attributed 145 of 528 acquisition events for Suede Voice to ChatGPT, about 27.5 percent, ahead of Explore at 99 and Search at 72. Suede publishes the breakdown in its Suede Voice case study. ChatGPT sent about twice as many as Play Search, and the note on measuring AI referrals shows how Suede tracks each source.

Most of what the lab produced is less dramatic: a long log of changes that did nothing. That log has its own write-up in GEO experiments that failed, and it tells clients what not to spend money on.

Does research on generative engines support this approach?

The academic work points the same way. The GEO paper by Aggarwal and colleagues, presented at KDD 2024, tested specific edits to source pages, such as adding citations, quotations and statistics. Some edits raised visibility in generative answers by up to about 40 percent on their benchmark, and others did little. The authors could only see that because they changed one thing at a time against a fixed set of queries. Suede's lab applies that discipline to live products instead of a benchmark, through what Suede calls the Suede iteration loop.

How does this reach client work?

Jason Colapietro's rule is that a tactic has to survive the lab before it goes near a client. If a change to a store description or an entity link moved how engines described an app, and the result repeated on a second app, it becomes a candidate for client work. If it only worked once, it stays in the lab. The path from finding to client playbook is covered in from app lab to client playbook.

Clients get the tactics without paying for the failures. They also get a straight answer when they ask why Suede recommends something: it was tested on an app Suede owns, and here is what changed.

Questions people ask about the app lab

Can a business with one website still test this way?

Partly. You can hold everything else still for a few weeks, change one section, and ask the same prompts before and after. You will get fewer data points than an app portfolio gives, so treat single results as hints.

Are the lab apps still live?

Many are. 17 are live in public stores: 9 on the Apple App Store, 6 on Google Play and 2 Chrome Web Store extensions. The iOS titles are listed on Jason Colapietro's Apple developer page.
